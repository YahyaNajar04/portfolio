A fire lookout sits alone on a peak for a season and watches. When smoke
appears they take a bearing, radio it in, and a crew goes out. The job is
mostly nothing happening, and the part that isn't nothing depends entirely on
whether you noticed.

I set it in the chir pine belt of the Shivaliks, 500 to 1,600 metres, because
the fire ecology there is unusually good raw material. *Pinus roxburghii* drops
something like 200,000 tonnes of needles a year across Jammu & Kashmir, and
that litter is the region's primary fuel — dry, continuous, and quick to carry
fire uphill. The fuel model wasn't invented for the game. It was already there.

## The atmosphere is the mechanic, not the art pass

The first thing I built was haze. Before a player controller, before the tower,
before anything you could walk on.

That sounds like getting the priorities backwards until you ask what the game
actually is. The core loop is *discriminating smoke from not-smoke at
distance*. If a column reads instantly at 25km the game has no skill in it; if
it never reads, there's no game. Everything hangs on the falloff curve between
those two failures, and that curve lives in depth fog with height falloff —
which is exactly why it had to come first. Atmosphere here isn't decoration
sitting on top of the design. It *is* the design.

Height-based fog does the specific work: haze pools in valleys and thins with
altitude, so ridges stay sharp while the low ground goes milky. That's also why
lookouts are on peaks — above the murk they'd otherwise be looking through.

## Godot and compasses disagree about which way is round

The Osborne Fire Finder is the instrument the whole job runs through: a
topographic map under glass, a rotating alidade, a rear peep sight and a front
sight with a vertical hairline. You put the wire on the smoke and read the
azimuth off a graduated ring.

Two lines carry the mechanic. The first converts a node rotation into a
bearing:

```gdscript
return fposmod(-rad_to_deg(alidade.rotation.y), 360.0)
```

Godot faces −Z and rotates counter-clockwise; a compass runs clockwise from
north. The negation flips the handedness and `fposmod` wraps past north instead
of handing back a negative bearing. Get it wrong and east reports as west —
which survives a long time as a bug, because the number still *looks* like a
bearing.

The second line is the one that makes it a game rather than an alignment
puzzle:

```gdscript
var offset_m := dist * tan(deg_to_rad(best_err))
```

That turns angular error into how far from the actual fire the crew ends up. At
15km, being off by a single degree puts them 262 metres out — through forest,
on a slope, while the fire grows. Precision stops being a score and becomes a
consequence, and I didn't have to design that. It's just trigonometry being
honest about what a lookout's job is.

## Authentic and playable are different numbers

The standard USFS L-4 cab is 14 by 14 feet. I built it at 4.3 metres and it
felt like standing in a wardrobe.

The reason is that a monitor at 75° FOV gives you a fraction of human
peripheral vision, so a room that's comfortable in life reads as a closet on
screen. The usual fix is widening the field of view — and I couldn't, because
wider FOV shrinks everything on screen including distant smoke. In most games
FOV is free. In this one it's the resource the entire mechanic is spending.

So the cabin grew instead, to 6.6 metres of interior. Larger than any real
lookout, and correct. The instrument is period-accurate to the inch and the
room around it is a lie, because those two things are answerable to different
masters.

## A forest is three different problems

Terrain3D's instancer renders hundreds of thousands of trees in a handful of
draw calls, and the documentation is blunt that they have no collision at all —
the meshes live on the GPU and the physics engine never hears about them. For a
game where the forest is a workplace you cut wood in, that's not a small gap.

It resolved into three tiers rather than one:

**Instanced trees** are visual density — the dark mass that makes distant smoke
hard to find. No collision, and none needed at a kilometre.

**Pooled collision proxies** give the near ones physical presence. A script
reads instance transforms out of the `MultiMeshInstance3D` nodes, and moves a
fixed pool of cylinder-shaped `StaticBody3D`s to the trees within 40m of the
player. Nothing is created or freed while you walk; bodies are recycled. A
player who walks into a tree registers *"I stopped,"* not the shape of the bark,
so a cylinder is the whole of what's required.

**Real scenes** are the trees you interact with — deadwood you can fell — which
need to be actual nodes because they need actual behaviour.

The cost of that architecture was a full day lost to a subtler thing: the
instancer takes the raw mesh resource and **ignores every transform in the
scene file**. Blender exports FBX as Z-up, Godot corrects it with a rotation on
the root node, and the instancer throws that correction away. The trees came
out lying flat. The fix is exporting glTF, which bakes orientation into the
vertices — and the lesson is that "the importer handles it" is only true until
something downstream reads past the importer.

## What it cost

It isn't shipped, and the honest status is prototype: the lookout duties work
end to end — sight, bearing, report, graded on accuracy — and the survival half
is mostly design document.

The scope roughly doubled partway through. I'd written the forest as somewhere
you occasionally hike to, and the game I actually wanted had chopping, water,
hunting and storage in it, which is a second game's worth of systems sharing a
map with the first. That was the right call and I'd make it again, but I made
it after building around the opposite assumption, and the design document had
to be rewritten rather than extended.

The unsolved problem is the one the game spends most of its time looking at.
Instanced forest reaches perhaps a kilometre; the horizon the player is scanning
is twenty-five. Distant terrain needs billboard impostors or baked backdrop
geometry, and I've done neither, which means the most important view in the game
is currently the least finished thing in it. The mechanic is proven at test
distances against placeholder columns. Proving it against a real horizon is
still ahead of me, and I'd rather say that than describe the prototype as the
game.
