A new preschool in Kishtwar had brochures, a fee sheet, and a WhatsApp number.
What they didn't have was anywhere to send a parent who asked "what do you
actually charge?" — and the parent asking was almost always on a phone, on a
connection that makes a 3 MB hero image a real cost.

## The boring framework was the right one

I know React. React is the wrong tool here. This is a content site — text,
prices, photos, a phone link — with no state worth managing and nothing that
reacts to anything. Shipping a runtime so that a fee table can render is a tax
paid by every parent on every visit.

Astro ships HTML. The framework disappears at build time, and what reaches the
phone is markup and a few kilobytes of hand-written JavaScript. The components
still feel like JSX, so nothing about authoring got worse.

## Content is data, and data has one home

Every changeable thing — fees, programs, staff bios, FAQ answers, the phone
number — lives in `src/data/*.json`, separate from the markup that renders it.
This isn't tidiness for its own sake. Monthly tuition appears on the home page
*and* the fees page; when the school raises UKG by ₹100, that's one number in
one file, not two templates to keep in sync and eventually forget to.

The same rule pushed the WhatsApp number into a single module that derives the
`wa.me` link, the `tel:` link, and the pre-filled enquiry message. It had been
hardcoded in four components before I noticed I'd be the one updating all four.

## The filesystem as the content interface

Photos don't get registered anywhere. A build-time `readdirSync` over
`public/gallery/` becomes the gallery; staff headshots resolve by deriving a
filename from the person's name and checking whether that file exists, falling
back to an illustrated avatar when it doesn't. Adding a photo means adding a
photo.

This is a deliberate trade of flexibility for a shorter instruction manual. The
school cannot reorder images arbitrarily — ordering is alphabetical, so you
prefix `01_`. In exchange, nobody has to be taught a schema.

## Hand-rolling the interactions, and what that taught me

The carousel, lightbox, and bio modals are vanilla JS with no dependencies. Two
things I would not have learned by installing a library:

`scrollLeft` rounds. An auto-scrolling strip that advances 0.5px per frame
never moves at all, because each frame reads back the rounded value and adds to
that. The fix is keeping the true position in your own float and pushing it
into the DOM — obvious afterwards, invisible beforehand.

And pointer events fight native scrolling on touch. My drag-to-scroll handler
worked beautifully on a trackpad and made the same strip nearly unusable on a
phone, because iOS momentum scrolling and my `requestAnimationFrame` loop were
both writing scroll position. Touch now falls through to the browser's own
scrolling entirely; the custom path is mouse-only. The lesson is that the
platform's implementation is better than mine and the work is knowing when to
get out of its way.

## What it cost

The site is statically built, so publishing a change means a rebuild — fine for
a school that revises fees once a year, wrong for anything with daily content.

The larger cost is unfinished: the JSON content layer was designed to sit under
a Git-based CMS so staff could edit fees and photos themselves. That isn't
shipped. Until it is, the school's self-service editing story is *"message the
developer"* — which is exactly the dependency the architecture was meant to
remove. The structure is right and the last mile is missing, and I'd rather say
that than describe the intent as the outcome.
