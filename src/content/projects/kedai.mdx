A convenience store needed two things from one app: a way for the owner to
manage stock, and a way for customers to check what's actually on the shelf
before walking over.

## One codebase, two roles

The obvious build is two apps, or one app with accounts for everyone. Both are
wrong here. A second app doubles the release surface for a single-store client.
And requiring customers to sign up to see whether milk is in stock is friction
that kills the only thing the app is for.

So: **accounts exist only for the owner.** Navigation is session-gated —
authenticated sessions get inventory management, everyone else gets a read-only
catalog. Customers never register.

## Authorization lives in the database, not the client

Session-gated navigation hides the management screens. It does not *protect*
them — anyone can point a client at the API. So the actual rule is enforced in
Postgres Row Level Security: the owner's session can write rows belonging to
their store, and nothing else can write at all. The client-side gating is a
convenience for the UI; RLS is the security boundary.

That distinction matters more than it sounds. A client check is a suggestion.
A row-level policy is a constraint the database enforces whether or not my code
is correct.

## Live stock, and the cost of it

Inventory changes propagate to customer devices in seconds over Supabase
Realtime, so the catalog reflects the shelf rather than the last app launch.
Product images upload from camera or gallery to Supabase Storage, with cleanup
for orphaned files when a product is removed — otherwise storage grows forever
with images nothing references.

The shared product catalog is self-seeding: generated-column normalisation plus
unique constraints deduplicate entries, so adding a product is one tap and the
catalog improves as it's used.

## What it cost

No customer accounts means no saved carts, no order history, no personalisation
— and no way to add them later without a migration through every row that
currently assumes an anonymous reader. That was the right trade for a
single-store client who needed adoption more than features. It would be the
wrong trade for a marketplace.
