581,012 cells in Roosevelt National Forest. 54 features — elevation, slope,
aspect, hillshade at three times of day, distance to water, roads and historic
fire points, plus one-hot wilderness area and soil type. Seven cover types, and
two of them account for most of the data.

## The comparison is the work

Three models is easy. Three models you can *honestly* compare is not, and it's
where most of the effort went.

**One split, shared.** Same `random_state`, same stratified 80/20, all three
models scored on the identical held-out set. Without that, the numbers aren't
comparable and the conclusion is decoration.

**Preprocessing inside the pipeline.** Every transformer lives in a
`ColumnTransformer` inside a `Pipeline`, so it refits per cross-validation fold
and never sees the validation data. Scaling happens for logistic regression and
not for the trees, because trees are scale-invariant and standardising them is
just wasted compute.

**Macro F1 as the selection criterion, not accuracy.** Classes 4 through 7 are
rare. A model that ignores them entirely still scores well on accuracy. Macro F1
weights every class equally, so the metric notices when the model quietly gives
up on the minority ones.

**Imbalance handled per model family** — `class_weight="balanced"` for logistic
regression and random forest, per-sample class weights for XGBoost, because
their APIs express the same idea differently.

## The result that's actually interesting

Tuned Random Forest scored *worse on accuracy* than my hand-picked baseline.

Two things cause that, and both are worth saying out loud. The "baseline" wasn't
scikit-learn defaults — it was a config I'd chosen deliberately, so the
comparison was really hand-tuning versus a randomised search. And the search
optimised macro F1, which buys minority-class recall by spending overall
accuracy. The tuned model is worse at the metric I wasn't optimising. That's the
trade working as intended, not a bug.

## What the model learned

Top features: wilderness area (0.125) and elevation (0.078), then soil types.
Which is the model rediscovering basic forest ecology — where you sit on the
mountain, and what you're sitting on, determines what grows. That agreement with
domain knowledge is a sanity check on the whole pipeline.

## What I'd tighten

The hyperparameter searches were thin — five draws from an eight-dimensional
space for XGBoost, run on half the training set. Logistic regression got a full
grid search on all the data, so the weakest model got the most thorough search
and the strongest got the least. The ranking is safe at a 30-point gap, but the
experiment doesn't establish it as cleanly as I'd want.

And the metrics only ever printed to stdout. Plots got saved; the numbers
didn't. A result you can't reload isn't a result.
